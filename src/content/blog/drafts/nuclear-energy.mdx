## Why?

Following the nuclear disaster at [Chernobyl]((The 1986 Chernobyl disaster in Ukraine was the worst nuclear accident in history, releasing massive amounts of radioactive material across Europe)), public sentiment around nuclear energy plummeted and governments were forced to take action. The data tells a stark story.

<MermaidDiagram code={`xychart-beta
    title "Nuclear Reactor Construction Starts Per Year"
    x-axis [1970, 1975, 1980, 1985, 1990, 1995, 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2020, 2023]
    y-axis "Reactors Started" 0 --> 30
    line [26, 25, 19, 11, 5, 1, 6, 3, 12, 7, 6, 4]

In the peak decade of the 1970s, we were starting **20-26 new reactors per year**. After Chernobyl in 1986 (when starts dropped from 11 to just 3), construction nearly ceased. By the mid-1990s, we were starting only **1 reactor per year globally**. There was a brief renaissance in the late 2000s, but we've never returned to anywhere near the 1970s pace.

Even more telling is *where* nuclear construction is happening today. Between 2015-2023, the cumulative capacity of new construction starts tells us everything:

<MermaidDiagram code={`xychart-beta
    title "Nuclear Construction by Country (2015-2023, MW)"

**China** dominates with 26,584 MW—more than the next three countries combined. The United States, which invented commercial nuclear power and once led the world, started just 1,250 MW over nine years. That's essentially one reactor.

Governments and international organizations also brought in much stricter regulation around the design and operation of plants. These new regulations certainly made nuclear power safer. Experts in the field consider modern nuclear plants to be basically unsinkable.

Contemporary reactor designs will automatically shut down—without requiring human intervention—in the event of a meltdown through [passive safety systems]((Modern reactors use gravity, natural circulation, and other physics-based safety features that don't require power or human action to prevent accidents)). They're also super clean. The amount of nuclear waste produced by a [1GW nuclear plant]((Roughly enough to power 750,000 homes)) over its 60-year lifetime is less than [20 cubic meters]((About the size of a small shipping container)) and can safely be stored in [deep geological repositories]((Underground facilities designed to isolate waste for thousands of years, such as Finland's Onkalo facility)).

The unfortunate cost of these developments is… financial.

## The Cost Problem

The capital required to bring a 1GW nuclear power station online is now approximately **$5.96 billion**. Compare that to:
- **$0.72 billion** for a gas turbine plant (8.3× cheaper)
- **$0.92 billion** for a combined-cycle gas plant (6.5× cheaper)
- **$1.15 billion** for a solar farm (5.2× cheaper)
- **$2.78 billion** for a coal plant (2.1× cheaper)

Nuclear does have one major advantage: [capacity factor]((The percentage of time a plant operates at full capacity; nuclear runs nearly constantly while solar/wind depend on weather)). Nuclear plants operate at 85% capacity year-round, compared to just 20% for solar and 30% for wind. This means a 1GW nuclear plant produces far more total energy than a 1GW solar farm.

<MermaidDiagram code={`xychart-beta

    y-axis "Capacity Factor (%)" 0 --> 90

However, even accounting for capacity factor, the economics remain challenging. When we look at LCOE—which factors in capacity factor, fuel, operations, and capital costs over the plant's lifetime—nuclear at $73.86/MWh is still more expensive than hydropower ($44.63), solar ($49.21), geothermal ($55.01), and wind onshore ($55.04).

This creates a paradox: we've made nuclear energy incredibly safe—arguably the safest form of power generation per unit of energy produced—but in doing so, we've made it economically unviable. Meanwhile, fossil fuels continue to release [greenhouse gases]((CO2 and methane that drive climate change)) and [particulate matter]((Fine particles that cause respiratory diseases and premature deaths)) that kill millions annually, yet remain the dominant energy source due to their low cost.

The question isn't whether nuclear energy is safe enough. It's whether we've optimized for the wrong metric entirely.
