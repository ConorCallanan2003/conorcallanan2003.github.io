On March 31, 1951 the first commercially available computer system was delivered to the U.S. Census Bureau. It represented the start of a transition that promised to revolutionise every aspect of the global economy. Looking back 75 years later, it’s clear that this was a reasonable promise.

It seems to me though, that we’re only now, a literal lifetime later, finally starting to approach an asymptote in efficiency gains to be made via digitization. Subjectively, it seems like what occurred was a kind of multi-stage S-curve, with the following distinct waves of adoption:

- Human computers (hand-calculations) are replaced by UNIVAC & IBM systems (governments and large companies)
- Calculation-heavy, but not logically complex systems are moved to mainframes (banking & finance)
- Complex information systems are digitized, allowing for greater operational complexity and organisational scaling (large corperations)
- SaaS companies and hyperscalers bring widespread digitization to consumers & SMEs
- (Current wave?) Lower costs and western market saturation pushes technology to developing economies (consumers & SMEs digitize before governments in some regions)

That puts approximately 50 years between the introduction of the first commerical computer systems and finally observing what looks to be some kind of market saturation. Before trying to glean learnings from this to apply to AI, it’s worth examing the causes of this pattern.

## Factors that influence rate of adoption

### Computational limitations

Some advancements had to wait until the technology caught up.

### Paradigmatic limitations

Object oriented programming made it feasible to model complex systems. A mature, standardised web made it possible to build mega-scaling SaaS products and 1bn+ user social networks. Paradigmatic advancements are often initially blocked by computational limitations, but are not guaranteed by computational advancements.

### Diffused knowledge & innovation

For companies to adopt new technologies and yield the promised benefits, you need people embedded in the right place, with the right knowledge.

### Public sentiment/concerns

If those who steward technology in the eyes of the public are seen to misuse it, the public grows understandably concerned and mistrustful. This slows adoptions and can lead to tightened regulation.

### Widespread awareness/adoption

Necessary for diffusion into consumer markets and as a day-to-day professional tool. Strongly connected to public sentiment and concerns.

### Regulatory & legislative interference

GDPR is the obvious example here, but lesser discussed regulations in areas like consumer banking have arguably been more detrimental (but seem to be easing a bit now).

### Limitations in public infrastructure

No consumer-facing hyperscaler (Facebook, Google, Netflix, etc.) could exist without widespread home broadband access.

### Limitations in public infrastructure (HARD)

This is _sort of_ a new limitation that’s emerged in the last two or three years as a result of the scale of recent AI infra investment. If buildout continues to grow at its current rate, it will soon start to outstrip the capacity of global energy production. This is a challenging problem to overcome (political, social, environmental, long-term), and is not one that the typical Silicon Valley approach is well-suited to. The say that this is a hard-fix is an outrageous understatement.

So how does this apply to AI Investment?

Well, the way I see it, _some_ of these limitations can be countered effectively with financial investment alone (albeit with diminishing returns). For example:

- Computational limitations
  - Apparently the answer is just to throw money at Nvidia
- Widespread awareness/adoption - ChatGPT made free-to-use (at a huge financial cost) - Students use it do assignments - White collar works use it to proofread emails
  - Massive marketing campaigns
    - Questionable efficacy here tbh
- Regulatory & legislative interference
  - Lobby goverments
  - Also requires good public sentiment
- Paradigmatic limitations
  - In-house research can accelerate development of AI engineer paradigms (MCP, agentic frameworks, tool-use standards)
  - This is working well but is maybe overly-centralized

The issue is that some of these can be countered minimally or not at all with greater investment:

- Diffused knowledge & innovation
  - This is extremely hard to do and has historically happened organicly and very slowly.
  - AI companies _have_ identified this as a concern and introduced the concept of a “Forward Deployed Engineer” as a possible solution. I’m sceptical that this can scale and that it will lead to long-term adoption. It feels like a recipe for customer churn.
- Public sentiment/concerns
  - Consumers are largely sceptical and fearful of AI. AI companies have done a terrible job of managing this thus far.

> It’s worth noting that this analysis doesn’t apply in a post-ASI world, because AIs would be capable of performing as drop-in replacements for humans in most white-collar jobs. FWIW I truly hope this does not happen.
