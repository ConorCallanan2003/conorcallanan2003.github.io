On March 31, 1951 the first commercially available computer system was delivered to the U.S. Census Bureau. It represented the start of a transition that promised to revolutionise every aspect of the global economy. Looking back 75 years later, it’s clear that this was a reasonable promise.

It seems to me though, that we’re only now, a literal lifetime later, finally starting to approach an asymptote in efficiency gains to be made via digitization. Subjectively, it seems like what occurred was a kind of multi-stage S-curve, with the following distinct waves of adoption:

- [Human computers]((individuals whose sole job it was to perform hand-calculations)) are replaced by UNIVAC & IBM systems (governments and large companies)
- Calculation-heavy, but not logically complex systems are moved to mainframes (banking & finance)
- Complex information systems are digitized, allowing for greater operational complexity and organisational scaling (large corperations)
- SaaS companies and hyperscalers bring widespread digitization to consumers & SMEs
- (Current wave?) Lower costs and western market saturation pushes technology to developing economies (consumers & SMEs digitize before governments in some regions)

That puts approximately 50 years between the introduction of the first commerical computer systems and finally observing what looks to be some kind of market saturation. In the interest of gleaning something from this to apply to our current situation, let's examine what influenced this pattern.

---

### What influenced the spread of digitisation?

##### Computational limitations

Simply put; some advancements had to wait until the technology caught up.

##### Paradigmatic limitations

Object oriented programming made it feasible to model complex systems. A mature, standardised web made it possible to build mega-scale SaaS products and 1bn+ user social networks. Paradigmatic advancements are often initially blocked by computational limitations, but are not guaranteed by computational advancements.

##### Diffused knowledge & innovation

For companies to adopt new technologies and yield the promised benefits, individuals must be embedded in the right place, with the right knowledge, and, crucially, [the right incentives]((long-term, sustainable results that benefit the company and align with its values)).

##### Public sentiment/concerns

If those who steward technology in the eyes of the public are seen to misuse it, the public grows understandably concerned and mistrustful. This slows adoptions and can lead to tightened regulation.

##### Widespread awareness/understanding

Necessary for diffusion into consumer markets and as a day-to-day professional tool. Strongly connected to public sentiment and concerns.

##### Regulatory & legislative interference

GDPR is the obvious example here, but lesser discussed regulations in areas like consumer banking have arguably been more detrimental (but seem to be easing a bit now).

##### Limitations in public infrastructure

No consumer-facing hyperscaler (Facebook, Google, Netflix, etc.) could exist without widespread home broadband access.

##### Limitations in public infrastructure (HARD)

This is _sort of_ a new limitation that’s emerged in the last two or three years as a result of the scale of recent AI infra investment. If buildout continues to grow at its current rate, it will soon start to outstrip the capacity of global energy production. 

This is a challenging problem to overcome, and is [not one that the typical Silicon Valley approach is well-suited to]((it would require long-term thinking, co-operation, consensus and awareness of social and environmental concerns)). To say that this is a hard fix is an outrageous understatement.

---

### How does this apply to AI Investment?

Well, the way I see it, _some_ of these limitations can be countered effectively with financial investment alone (albeit with diminishing returns). This includes:

- [Computational limitations]((continue to throw money at nvidia))

- [Widespread awareness/understanding]((make consumer facing products free and spend big on marketing campaigns)) 
    
- [Regulatory & legislative interference]((can be achieve via strong lobbying, requires positive public sentiment however))

- [Paradigmatic limitations]((in-house research accelerates development & adoption of new paradigms like MCP, agentic frameworks, and industry standards. brings with it a risk of over-centralization))

The issue is that some of these can be countered minimally or not at all with greater investment:

- [Diffused knowledge & innovation]((extremely hard to do and has historically happened organicly and very slowly.))
  - A note on [Forward Deployed Engineers]((FDEs have been proposed as a possible solution to this issue. i’m sceptical that the concept scales effectively and feel there's a risk of churn when they "un-deploy" from the customer.))
- [Public sentiment/concerns]((Consumers are largely sceptical and fearful of AI. AI companies have done a terrible job of managing this issue thus far.))

---

### What does all of this mean?
- Companies are trying to solve issues that don't scale with capital investment, with **MASSIVE** capital investment.
- We're currently in an environment of widespread misguided and unsustainable investment. 
- We **are** in a bubble. It **will** burst.
- The quiet adoption of private credit financing by the [hyperscalers]((meta, aws, google, etc.)) will widen the fallout.
- Companies that don't over-capitalize now and have solid fundamentals will have an opportunity to [pick up the pieces after the crash]((*and grow into the exponential growth phase of the S-curve)).


---

> I want to add that this analysis doesn’t apply in a post-ASI world, because AIs would be capable of performing as drop-in replacements for humans in most white-collar jobs. FWIW I hope this does not happen.
